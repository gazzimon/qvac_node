In one line

All four papers leave the same box empty: ownerless matching. When we went looking for more, a model had to invent the missing papers. That confirms it.

1 · The central finding

The four papers come from independent groups and from different disciplines: security (PolyU Hong Kong), distributed systems (UC Berkeley), auction theory (Exeter), and control theory (Oxford + FLock.io). None cites the others. And all four, by different paths, end up in the same place.

The three papers that have to match a request to a machine leave that role centralized — and two admit it in writing.

PolyLink runs its API server, which batches and routes on a 4-core cloud instance. DeServe says, in Open Problems: "matching compute resources with tasks remains centralized". AERIA doesn't leave it centralized by oversight: the auctioneer is the design, and the paper details exactly what it needs to know to work — each user's budget, their latency requirement, their accuracy requirement, and which model they're requesting.

That last observation is the most valuable finding of the study. AERIA doesn't describe an accidental privacy leak: it describes the complete profile an inference-market coordinator needs as input. It can't be encrypted, because the algorithm has to read it. It can't be avoided, because without it there's no allocation. It can only be moved to a place where no one sees it.

It's the argument already written in PyrusLLM-application.md: "You cannot have an ownerless market if the piece that decides and charges belongs to someone". The difference is that now there are three independent states of the art confirming it, one with the technical detail of exactly what data gets exposed.

The three bottom layers are empty across all four papers. It's not that they solve them poorly: they don't attempt them. There's a real tension there (anti-DoS versus non-linkability) that none of the four sees.

4 · The fabrication episode

A second round of search, with a different model, returned a report that looked impeccable: prestigious venues, DOIs, repos, numbers to two decimal places, an evidence-quality table, and even a section on contradictions between papers.

I verified four citations. Three were fabricated.

Citation in the reportWhat's actually at that identifier
2502.04112 — "PoEx: Verifiable Token Metering"Estimation of large approximate dynamic matrix factor models — econometrics
2411.05210 — "Benchmarking CC on H100"Algorithmic Autonomy in Data-Driven AI — algorithm ethics
2502.11890 — "Economics of Consumer GPU"Revisiting Classification Taxonomy for Grammatical Errors — linguistics
2406.18665 — RouteLLMReal, with the title and the numbers changed

The identifiers existed. They pointed to real papers. None was the paper described. It's the hardest kind to catch: if you only checked that the link resolved, it passed.

The three patterns visible without even opening the links

  • The repos. pride-anon/…, fastbid-anon/…, poex-project/poex-core, volatileserve/vserve. The -anon suffix belongs to anonymized submissions — it doesn't survive as a public repo. And the rest follow a template: paper-name slash paper-name.
  • An impossible venue. "VolatileServe — PETS 2025." PETS is the privacy-technologies symposium, and the work described is speculative scheduling: zero privacy content.
  • Real technology, false citation. RLN is real and its repo belongs to the Ethereum Foundation — but it's not an IEEE S&P 2024 paper. That pattern is worse than inventing everything: the verifiable part convinces you of the part that isn't.

The rule that comes out of this, and that holds beyond this study: a link that resolves is not verification. You have to read the title.

This is where the previous episode stops being an anecdote and becomes the strongest finding of the study. The fabrications didn't turn up just anywhere: they turned up exactly on the five questions the first search had declared empty.

A web-connected search said "there's nothing." Another model had to invent it. A model hallucinates where there's nothing to retrieve, so fabrication marks the gap with the same precision as absence — and it's a signal independent of the first.

For the pitch, that translates into a sentence that can now be defended: it's not that we didn't search well. It doesn't exist. Three of those five gaps are boxes on the product.

7 · Cross-cutting conclusions

1. Naive sharding is dead; pipelining works, but only in batch

PolyLink splits a 14B model across two GPUs and gets 7.12 tok/s with 160 s of latency, versus 55.73 tok/s and 17.3 s on a single 4090. DeServe splits a 70B model across the two US coasts and gets 445 tok/s, flat from 0 to 256 ms. The difference isn't the network: it's the queue. What engineering recovers is throughput; what nothing recovers is per-request latency.

2. The batch lane shows up twice, by independent paths

DeServe argues for it from systems: it gave up competing on latency. AERIA produces it solely from the auction: since dₓ = βₓ/āₓ and āₓ drops as the deadline extends, the patient user wins more often and more cheaply. The discount for waiting doesn't need to be wired in.

3. The batch lane resolves the main technical risk

The Vela application leaves open that "a round trip to the enclave per request may not be viable". AERIA clears ~100 bids in 3 ms, and Cryptobazaar does 128 sealed bidders in <0.5 s. In batch, with an SLA measured in hours, that's noise. The pattern: clearing by window, not matching by request.

4. The supply segment the application targets isn't the one that pencils out

Using DeServe's table at 0.90 USD/MTok: it takes 4,283 tok/s at GCP pricing, 1,139 at io.net pricing, and 108 at mining-opportunity rates. Their system does 445. It only pencils out at mining opportunity cost. Running the numbers: ~0.044 USD/GPU-hour, a ~1,800 USD 4090 takes ~41,000 hours — 4.7 years — to pay back. Buying hardware for this doesn't pay off.

5. Verifying identity and verifying quality are two different problems

TIQE's judge prompt says "rate the quality of the following LLM inference result" — it never mentions which model was supposed to run. A worker that advertises 14B and serves 1.5B with decent answers scores a 5. That's why the cross-encoder catches only 66 %. And the LLM-judge, which reaches 98 %, has 12 % false positives — with R = α(1−β)R_batch, one honest batch in eight gets punished.

6. Structural constraints beat calibrated parameters

Under sustained attack, the PID with hard bounds contains adversarial success at 4.2 %; the adversarially trained one, at 12.5 %; the one calibrated by grid search gets drained 94.1 % of the time. This retroactively explains PolyLink's worst flaw: its validator reward is β + (1−α)(1−β), strictly decreasing in quality, and slashing only punishes deviating from the median. A bound of the form "no role gets paid more when quality drops" would have made that formula impossible to write.

7. Token metering is a measured fraud, not a hypothesis

This is the second stage's contribution, and it's the most actionable. Token Inflation measures 1,469 % average undetected inflation in reasoning tokens, and 50.85 % exploiting tokenization ambiguity: an honest charge of USD 100 turned into USD 1,569 on the same query. And its conclusion names the way out: "evidence outside provider control, such as trusted execution attestation".

The project roadmap had already named the hole — "a node that reports 900 tokens when it served 400 has no one to contradict it today". Now it has a citation, a number, and a named solution that lines up with Track V.

8. Confidential GPU inference stopped being aspirational

4–8 % throughput overhead on H100 in confidential computing mode; <7 % for typical queries, and the bottleneck is PCIe, not compute. With two caveats that need to be said in the same breath: HBM memory is unencrypted, and so is NVLink between GPUs. For a single GPU the threat model holds; for a distributed model, it doesn't. And a third, independent one: inter-token timing leaks length and structure even when everything else is encrypted.

8 · Where the study corrects me

The first version of this document recommended known-answer probes as the response to the problem of "a node declares which model it runs, and that declaration has to be provable". The literature bounds that, and it's worth saying so before building it.

What you want to detectDoes the probe work?What covers it instead
Advertises 14B, serves 1.5BYes — fingerprinting worksProbes (Phase 10.5)
Same model, more quantizedNo — no published black-box solutionSigned declaration + stake + arbitration

In other words: quantization is covered economically, not technically. That's exactly the role DeServe gives optimistic verification, and it's more honest than promising a detection that nobody currently knows how to do. It stays as D26 in the roadmap.

A methodological caveat: the citation this rests on (2504.04715) is the only one in the study I didn't verify, and a scope decision depends on it. It needs to be opened before committing to Fase 10.5.

9 · What to build first

Ordered by: low cost × unlocks a lot × impossible to add later. The phase-by-phase detail is in ROADMAP_REVISION-EVIDENCIA.md.

01

Provider attestation on the receipt

+0.5 daycan't be retrofitted

Fase 10 rightly says that "the EIP-3009 signature is already the receipt." But it covers only one direction: the client authorizing payment. Missing is the provider attesting to what it served — model_id, quantization, hash(prompt), hash(output), prefill and decode tokens, nonce, signed with the wallet.

And it's exactly the exploitable direction. Token Inflation measures it at 1,469 %. The code already assumes it: recordPeerResult takes the node's word for it.

Exactly when it becomes exploitable: not in Fase 9 — D9 charges a fixed, declared cap. In Fase 10, once the batch accumulates real consumption. That's why the artifact is born in 9: D12 already requires building the receipt as a final SSE event, and hanging fields off it costs a fraction of building it separately. Requests that already went through don't get signed retroactively.

02

Separate prefill from decode in metering

+0.5 day

Prefill is compute-bound and parallel; decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and sequential; the KV cache holds memory proportional to context for the whole generation. A single rate blends three costs that don't scale the same way.

Log it now, price it later. D22 stays untouched; pushLog gains fields. Before Fase 10 freezes accounting into a single dimension.

03

Model-identity probes

~1 dayscope bounded by D26

Known-answer prompts injected by the gateway, indistinguishable from real traffic because the request already arrives decoupled from identity. PolyLink can't do this: its API server is visible and the worker knows exactly where everything comes from.

Your privacy layer is what makes verification possible — and that deserves to be written into the Vela application, because it's a design argument, not a coincidence.

04

Reserve price per provider

minimal cost

A floor (1+γ)·cost tied to the local electricity rate. It's the cheapest piece of AERIA, and neither PolyLink nor DeServe has it. Without a floor, a provider serves at a loss during a rate spike and leaves — which is the churn DCBM measures as the death metric for these networks.

05

Window clearing inside the enclave

depends on Velathe differentiator

This is your product, but it depends on an external timeline. The four previous points are its inputs: without attestation there's nothing to recompute, without metering there's nothing to bill, without probes there's no reputation, without a floor there's no constraint. Doing them first means that the day Vela launches, the enclave has everything it needs.

06

Model cascade

biggest margin impact

UCCI: 31 % savings (95 % CI: 27–35) over 75,000 real queries on H100, with code. Cross-referenced with DeServe — 445 tok/s against a break-even of 1,139 at rental price — the cheapest way to serve a large model is to not run it on a third of the requests.

Comes after Fase 11.5, which is not optional.

10 · What has to be decided

The three that block progress

D1 · What is the operating economy denominated in?

Everything in $QVAC — or a stablecoin payment rail with the native token only for incentives.

Recommendation — no empirical evidence, this is an argumentSeparate them. The provider gets paid in stable, because they plan against their electricity bill. DCBM's entire apparatus exists to solve a problem created by denominating the real economy in a volatile asset. Caveat: neither search found a single study comparing operator retention between the two options — and the second one invented one. Present this as reasoning, not as data.

D2 · Who is the target provider?

Investors who buy hardware — or owners of already-idle rigs.

RecommendationIdle rigs. Buying a GPU for this takes ~4.7 years to pay back. Today the application targets the segment that doesn't pencil out.

D3 · Optimistic verification or committee?

A staked committee that scores every batch — or signature + stake + arbitration only on dispute.

Recommendation — reinforcedOptimistic, with the enclave as arbiter. PolyLink permanently reserves 30 % of all revenue for validators. And Token Inflation shows the committee doesn't solve metering either: scoring quality doesn't catch an inflated count.

The four design decisions

D4 · One lane or two?

Interactive only — or interactive + batch.

RecommendationTwo, and launch confidential matching in batch first. Batch tolerates intermittent hardware, which is the nature of idle rigs.

D5 · Does quality affect payment or routing?

Per-request multiplier — or EWMA reputation that weights routing.

RecommendationRouting. With 12 % false positives, a per-batch multiplier is pure variance on an honest provider's income. A bad provider loses traffic, not money per request.

D6 · What's the unit of resource?

Allocated FLOPS — flat tokens — or prefill, decode, and memory occupancy.

Recommendation — refinedThe third. AERIA's model assumes a single forward pass and doesn't transfer. But plain "tokens" isn't enough either: it's input tokens, output tokens, and memory occupancy over duration. Log them now; price them once you have your own data.

D7 · What does your auction maximize?

Intermediary revenue — compute-provider revenue — or total welfare.

RecommendationDecide it and publish it. The mechanism works with any objective; AERIA plugs in "auctioneer revenue," which turns it into a seller extracting surplus. With the auction inside the enclave and the code published, anyone can verify which objective you're maximizing. Nobody else has that.

11 · What not to build

Naive cross-device sharding160 s and 7.12 tok/s for a 14B model, versus 17.3 s on a single 4090.
Permanent validator committee30 % of revenue, plus three attack surfaces — and it still doesn't solve metering.
A homegrown cryptographic metering schemeThe scheme with 2.3 % overhead that made the rounds doesn't exist: that identifier is an econometrics paper. The real path is the enclave (V2).
PID stabilization of the tokenThe theorem itself forbids it at your scale: with α = 2/y, a thin pool sends the controller into the unstable region.
ZK proofs of inferencezkLLM: 803 s per proof, 986 s of commitment for a 13B model.
A serving engine from scratchCoLearn-Dev/deserve is a starting point — research code from January 2025, verify before relying on it.

DeServe's three disqualification criteria

Worth adopting as a standing filter for any verification mechanism:

  1. Does it load the provider with heavy compute? (kills opML, spML, and zkLLM)
  2. Does it put inputs or outputs on-chain? Gas can exceed the cost of inference. (kills opML)
  3. Does it force the provider to answer challenges from anyone? It's a DoS vector. (kills opML)
